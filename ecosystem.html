The Ecosystem

How the pieces fit together – and why they were built this way.

Different machines – Same story

A packaging line, a fed-batch bioreactor, a pharmaceutical freeze-dryer, etc.

The freeze-dryer, for instance. The pump load was drifting from batch 17 . The deviation hit at batch 50 .
But the signal was there the whole time…

Two types of signals

Discrete events – alarms on/off, valve open/closed, operator entries…, timestamped.
This is what audit trails are made of. That’s what machinelogR reads.

Continuous measurements – temperature, pressure, weight… over time.
This is typically what historians store. That’s what processlogR will handle.

Different data. Same logic. Two sister packages.

Architecture

A few examples of functions:

alarm_pareto() counts alarm frequencies.
spc_chart() plots a control chart.
lyo_batch() simulates a freeze-drying batch.

A function, a simulator, and a grammar rule are all bricks.
Bricks snap together into walls. Walls build houses – a CPV report, a maintenance dashboard, an audit trail review.

→ →

The same brick, like spc_chart(), may appear in different houses.
The context changes. The function does not.

The datasets

All datasets used here are either open data or generated on this site.

PIADE (Packaging Industry Anomaly DEtection) is a real dataset – packaging line logs published as open data on Kaggle. That’s what I used to build and test machinelogR on actual industrial data.

FDSim is a freeze-dryer simulator I built for this site. You can see it in the Toolbox: the deviation at batch 50, the pump signal at batch 17.

IndPenSim (Industrial-scale Penicillin Simulation) is a public simulator for fed-batch bioreactors (industrialpenicillinsimulation.com). I use it to build and validate processlogR, and as a showcase for continuous bioprocess data.

Real data from real plants exists. It won’t appear here – but the tools are built to read it.
